Version 2

Refined After User Testing

Informed by real student sessions at the California School for the Blind, V2 removes the line blocker and introduces a pressure-based end-of-line sensor.

Design Changes

Built around how students actually read

Testing V1 with students at the California School for the Blind revealed that the line blocker — while well-intentioned — disrupted the students' natural reading posture. V2 was redesigned from the ground up based on that direct student feedback.

Line Blocker Removed

Student testing showed that the line blocker interfered with natural finger movement and reading position. V2 removes it entirely, letting students read completely unobstructed.

Based on CSB student testing
New Sensor: Pressure / Linear Potentiometer

Instead of mid-line proximity detection, V2 detects when a student presses the end-of-line marker. The time difference between consecutive end-of-line touches gives the reading time for each line.

Hardware redesign
Non-Slip Mat Added

Paper movement during sessions was a recurring issue in V1. A non-slip mat is now placed under the Braille page to keep it stationary throughout the entire reading session.

Usability fix from V1

How It Works

End-of-line touch timing

V2 measures reading speed by detecting when the student touches the end of each line — no mid-read interruptions, no guides.

1
Place Paper on the Non-Slip Mat
The Braille page is placed on the non-slip mat, keeping it stable and preventing any drift during the session.
2
Read Naturally — No Guide Required
The student reads each Braille line without any line blocker or physical guide, preserving their natural reading posture and tactile experience.
3
Tap the End-of-Line Sensor
At the end of each line, the student taps the linear potentiometer / pressure sensor. The device logs a timestamp marking that line's completion.
4
Time Difference = Line Speed
The difference between consecutive tap timestamps gives the reading time for each line, calculated using the standard 40-characters-per-line convention.
5
Data Sent to Google Sheets
Line-by-line timing data is uploaded as a CSV to a hotspot webpage, where a Google Apps Script automatically generates fluency graphs for teachers.

What We Learned

Insights that led to Version 3

The Limits of Hardware

V2 resolved the key issues from V1, but still only captured line-level timing data. Teachers could see how long a student spent on each line, but not where within a line they struggled, whether they were re-reading sections, or which specific words were slowing them down. A more granular approach was needed — and that meant moving beyond hardware.
